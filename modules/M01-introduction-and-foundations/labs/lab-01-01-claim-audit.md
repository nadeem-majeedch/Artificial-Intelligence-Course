## Scenario

The student services office at a mid-sized university receives about 1,400 emails a day:
registration problems, fee queries, timetable clashes, and everything that does not fit. A
vendor offers an "AI enquiry assistant" that routes each email to one of four queues. The
proposal says: *"96% accuracy on real university enquiries, deployed at three institutions."*

The head of student services has one working day to decide. She is not technical, she has been
burned before by a reporting tool that "worked in the demo", and she must defend the decision to
a committee that includes the student union. She asks you: **is the number 96% meaningful for
our mail, and what would we be accepting if we bought it?**

## Your role

You are the data analyst contracted for one day. You report to the head of student services and
you will be judged on whether your evidence table lets a non-technical reader make a decision —
not on whether you can build the most accurate classifier.

## What you are given

| The vendor claim | this page | 96% accuracy, three unnamed institutions |
| Evaluation harness | [NB-01-01](../notebooks/nb-01-01-capability-test-bench.ipynb) | accuracy, precision, recall, calibration |

**Not provided:** the vendor's test set, their label definitions, their error costs, or any
information about which three institutions. Those gaps are part of the exercise — record them as
UNKNOWN rather than inventing values.

## Requirements

**Functional**

- R1 — Compute the **majority-class baseline** for the corpus and report it alongside any accuracy you quote.
- R2 — Implement one rule-based keyword classifier and report **per-class precision and recall**, not only accuracy.
- R3 — Produce a **calibration table** (three confidence buckets) from your classifier's scores.
- R4 — Translate the claimed error rate into **errors per working day** at 1,400 emails, with a stated cost model.
- R5 — Fill the six-field **claim audit table** from L01-04 §4, using `UNKNOWN` where evidence is missing.

**Non-functional**

- N1 — Runs in under 10 seconds on a CPU laptop, standard library only.
- N2 — Reproducible: fixed corpus, no randomness, same output every run.
- N3 — Every number in your report is produced by your committed script; none is typed by hand.

## Tasks

1. **Build and inspect the corpus.** Generate the fixed corpus below and print class counts.
   *(success: you can state the majority-class baseline and the smallest class size)*

   The corpus is synthetic but not tidy: about half the emails mention a second topic
   ("cannot register for CS101 — also paid the fee last week"). Real student mail does this, and
   it is the main reason a keyword classifier fails. Do not edit the generator; everyone must
   work on identical data.

   ```python
   import random
   from collections import Counter

   random.seed(7)
   TEMPLATES = {
       "registration": ["cannot register for {course}", "registration portal rejects {course}",
                        "need to add {course} before the deadline", "enrolment error for {course}"],
       "fees": ["fee statement shows {amount} outstanding", "payment for {course} not reflected",
                "instalment plan question for {course}", "refund still pending after {weeks} weeks"],
       "timetable": ["two lectures for {course} clash", "tutorial group changed for {course}",
                     "exam timetable missing {course}", "room change for {course} not showing"],
       "other": ["library access expired", "wifi keeps dropping in the lab",
                 "request for a transcript", "student card replacement"],
   }
   SECONDARY = {
       "registration": ["also paid the fee last week", "timetable clash as well"],
       "fees": ["and I still cannot register", "the lecture room is not listed"],
       "timetable": ["fee receipt attached", "registration is still pending"],
       "other": ["exam timetable unclear", "fee question as well"],
   }
   COURSES = ["CS101", "MA102", "AI201", "SE210", "DS220"]
   COUNTS = {"registration": 96, "fees": 72, "timetable": 48, "other": 24}

   corpus = []
   for intent, n in COUNTS.items():
       for i in range(n):
           template = TEMPLATES[intent][i % len(TEMPLATES[intent])]
           text = template.format(course=random.choice(COURSES), amount=random.randint(200, 3000),
                                  weeks=random.randint(1, 6))
           if random.random() < 0.5:
               text += " - " + random.choice(SECONDARY[intent])
           corpus.append((text, intent))
   random.shuffle(corpus)
   print(Counter(label for _, label in corpus))
   print(corpus[0])
   ```

2. **Compute the baseline.** Report the accuracy of always predicting the largest class. Compare
   it with 96%. *(success: you can explain why a 96% claim needs a baseline before it means anything)*

3. **Write the keyword classifier.** Four keyword lists, one per queue; unfound keywords fall back
   to `other`. Report accuracy, and per-class precision and recall in a table. *(success: your table
   shows at least one class where recall and precision differ by more than 0.2)*

4. **Calibrate it.** Score each email as the fraction of its words that matched the chosen class's
   keywords, bucket the scores (0–0.33, 0.34–0.66, 0.67–1.0), and compute observed accuracy per
   bucket plus ECE. *(success: you can say whether confidence is usable as a routing threshold)*

5. **Cost the errors.** At 1,400 emails a day, compute the number of misrouted emails implied by
   (a) the vendor's 96%, (b) your classifier. Assume 40% of misroutes cost a student one day of
   delay and 5% of those require staff intervention of 15 minutes. Report staff-hours per week and
   name the harm that is *not* captured by that number.

6. **Write the verdict.** Fill the six-field audit table, then write three sentences to the head of
   student services: what the number means, what is unknown, and what you would require before a
   pilot.

## Extension challenges

1. **Change the prevalence.** Rebalance the corpus so `other` is 60% of all mail. Which metric moves
   most, and why? Does the vendor's number become more or less meaningful?
2. **Adversarial wording.** Add 20 emails whose keywords point to the wrong queue ("my fee payment
   for the timetable module…"). Measure the damage to precision. What would a learned classifier
   need in order to do better?
3. **Human baseline.** Ask two classmates to label 30 emails. Compute their agreement and treat the
   disagreement as a labelling error ceiling. What does that ceiling imply for any 96% claim?

## Hints

<details><summary>Hint 1 — getting started</summary>

Run Task 1 exactly as written before changing anything. Print `len(corpus)` and the class counts;
if they do not match `COUNTS`, you have edited the generator and your numbers will not be
comparable with anyone else's.

</details>

<details><summary>Hint 2 — the likely sticking point</summary>

Precision and recall are computed *per class*, and they use different denominators: precision
divides by everything you predicted as that class, recall divides by everything that truly is that
class. With four classes, a confusion matrix is the least error-prone way to get both.

</details>

<details><summary>Hint 3 — checking your work</summary>

Accuracy must equal the trace of the confusion matrix divided by the total. If your hand-computed
accuracy and the trace disagree, you have a bug in the matrix, not in the classifier.

</details>
